# Concurrent training (swim + gym), the interference effect, and dryland strength training for a masters swimmer

Scope: one recreational/masters swimmer, ~50+ years old; 3 swims/week of 65–80 min; ~2 gym sessions/week of 45–60 min. Health filters (applied, not researched): right hip Perthes sequelae (no running or jumping, loaded squat no deeper than 90°, breaststroke ≤10% of monthly volume); right shoulder rotator cuff/impingement; right knee chondromalacia.

Method note: PubMed, PMC, Springer, Frontiers and several mirrors were blocked by the network proxy, so no full texts could be opened. Every figure below comes from abstracts or search-engine excerpts of the primary papers. Where a number appeared only in a secondary summary or blog, it is marked "(secondary, unverified)". Subgroup statistics should be checked against the full text before anyone quotes them as exact.

---

## Q1. What do meta-analyses (2012–2026) say about interference with strength, power, hypertrophy and endurance, and does it matter for swimming?

### Takeaway
Current meta-analyses agree that adding endurance work does not meaningfully blunt **maximal strength or hypertrophy** in untrained or moderately trained people. The interference that does show up is mainly in:
- **explosive strength/power**, especially when both modes are done in the same session;
- **lower-body maximal strength** in trained people and in men.

Upper-body strength appears unaffected, and VO2max gains are not impaired in trained people. For a 50+ recreational swimmer who mainly wants strength and health rather than maximal power, the practical interference risk is small, provided sessions are spaced apart and endurance volume is moderate.

### Cited Findings

**Wilson et al. 2012** (JSCR 26(8):2293–2307; 21 studies, 422 effect sizes)
- Endurance-training **frequency** correlated negatively with hypertrophy, strength and power gains (r ≈ −0.26 to −0.35), and so did endurance **duration** (r ≈ −0.29 to −0.75).
- Conclusion: interference "is a factor of the modality, frequency, and duration of the endurance training selected." — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51719597_Concurrent_Training_A_Meta-Analysis_Examining_Interference_of_Aerobic_and_Resistance_Exercises); [Academia.edu copy](https://www.academia.edu/103997237/Concurrent_Training)
- The specific claim that running interfered more than cycling (hypertrophy ES 0.85 vs 1.23) appeared only in an AI-generated summary and could **not** be verified. — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/72153271/Concurrent_Training)

**Schumann et al. 2022** (Sports Med 52:601–612; 43 studies; updated systematic review and meta-analysis)
- Concurrent aerobic and strength training "does not compromise muscle hypertrophy and maximal strength development."
- Explosive strength gains may be attenuated. The attenuation was significant when both modes were done **in the same session** (p = 0.043) and not significant when sessions were **separated by at least 3 h** (p > 0.05). — [Search summary / sportrxiv preprint](https://sportrxiv.org/index.php/server/preprint/download/37/71/54); [JCU record](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/)
- No significant moderation by mode (cycling vs running), frequency (more vs fewer than 5 sessions/week), training status, or **mean age (<40 vs >40 years)**. — [same](https://portfolio.jcu.edu.au/publications/JCU174136)
- Pooled SMDs: maximal strength −0.06, hypertrophy −0.01 (secondary, unverified). — [getfitcraft summary](https://getfitcraft.com/science/concurrent-training-interference)

**Petré et al. 2021** (Sports Med 51:991–1010; 27 studies; lower-body 1RM; concurrent vs resistance-only)
- Effect sizes by training status: trained ES = −0.35 (p < 0.01); moderately trained ES = −0.20 (p = 0.08, not significant); untrained ES = 0.03 (p = 0.87). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/350289319_Development_of_Maximal_Dynamic_Strength_During_Concurrent_Resistance_and_Endurance_Training_in_Untrained_Moderately_Trained_and_Trained_Individuals_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-analysis)
- Within the trained group, interference concentrated in same-session training (ES −0.66) and shrank to −0.10 (p = 0.55) when sessions were separated by hours or days (secondary, unverified). — [getfitcraft](https://getfitcraft.com/science/concurrent-training-interference)

**Huiberts, Wüst & van der Zwaard 2023/2024** (Sports Med; reported as 59 studies and 1,346 participants, secondary)
- Lower-body strength gains were blunted in **males** (SMD ≈ −0.43) but **not in females** (SMD ≈ 0.08); sex difference p = 0.03.
- VO2max gains were impaired only in **untrained** participants (SMD −0.35), not in trained or highly trained ones.
- **Upper-body strength adaptations were unchanged.** Power gains were mildly blunted in both sexes (secondary). — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374781428_Concurrent_Strength_and_Endurance_Training_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_on_the_Impact_of_Sex_and_Training_Status); [Amsterdam UMC PDF](https://pure.amsterdamumc.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/141008429/Concurrent-strength-and-endurance-training.pdf)

**Held et al. 2026** (Sports Med, doi 10.1007/s40279-026-02401-y; umbrella review of meta-analyses, reported as 17 meta-analyses, 144 studies and 1,492 participants, secondary)
- Concurrent training gave aerobic gains comparable to endurance-only training, and strength and hypertrophy "relatively similar" to resistance-only training.
- Power adaptations were **significantly lower** than with resistance-only training. — [Omnicuris summary](https://www.omnicuris.com/medshots/daily_updates/concurrent-training-adaptations-umbrella-review); [Springer landing](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-026-02401-y)
- Secondary sources attribute the power interference mainly to accumulated fatigue and suggest separating sessions by 6–24 h. These look like practitioner extrapolations, not pooled results. — [Omnicuris](https://www.omnicuris.com/medshots/daily_updates/concurrent-training-adaptations-umbrella-review)

**Mechanism reviews**
- Fyfe, Bishop & Stepto 2014 (Sports Med 44:743–762): AMPK activated by endurance exercise can inhibit mTORC1 through TSC2 and MuRF-1/MAFbx/ULK1. However, human studies "have not observed such molecular effects" consistently. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40279-014-0162-1); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261610150_Interference_between_Concurrent_Resistance_and_Endurance_Exercise_Molecular_Bases_and_the_Role_of_Individual_Training_Variables)
- Coffey & Hawley 2017 (J Physiol 595:2883–2896): interference comes from "multiple integrated, rather than isolated, effectors". Sequencing, scheduling, volume, intensity and recovery can mitigate it, and training status is a key moderator. — [Bond University](https://research.bond.edu.au/en/publications/concurrent-exercise-training-do-opposites-distract); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/306041701_Concurrent_exercise_training_Do_opposites_distract)

**Middle-aged and older adults**
- Markov et al. 2022 (Sports Med; ages 50–73): concurrent training vs passive controls for strength, power, balance and cardiorespiratory fitness.
- A separate meta-analysis in middle-aged to older adults found that concurrent training gave greater upper- and lower-body strength than aerobic-only training, equal cardiorespiratory fitness gains, and no reduction compared with either single mode. — [Search summary incl. PMC9876872](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9876872/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031938422001949)
- A 2025 meta-analysis (MDPI Healthcare 13(7):776; split into 50–<65 and ≥65 years) found greater lean mass with concurrent than with aerobic-only training. — [MDPI](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/13/7/776)

### Inferences
- **Swimming vs running/cycling.** No meta-analysis isolates swimming as the endurance mode; all pooled data come from running and cycling. Two points can be extrapolated, with low certainty:
  - Swimming is non-weight-bearing and low-eccentric, like cycling, so it should impose little lower-body muscle damage. Lower-body gym work should therefore see minimal interference.
  - Swimming is upper-body dominant, so the "local" overlap moves to the shoulders, lats and triceps. The meta-analyses say upper-body strength is not blunted, but those studies used leg-based endurance work, so this does not carry over directly.
- **The real risk for this user is local fatigue and overuse in the shoulder, not molecular interference.** The acute data under Q2 (internal-rotator torque down ~23% after a hard swim set) support treating hard swim days and heavy upper-body pulling or pressing as competing for the same tissue.
- **The user's profile sits in the low-risk zone.** He is recreational (untrained to moderately trained in strength), over 40, and aims mainly at strength and hypertrophy rather than power. Age >40 did not moderate interference in Schumann 2022. Male sex is a modest risk factor for lower-body strength blunting (Huiberts).
- **Volume matters.** Wilson 2012's negative frequency and duration correlations suggest keeping total swim volume moderate, which the planned 3 × 65–80 min already is, and avoiding added endurance sessions on gym days.

### Gaps
- No RCT or meta-analysis of concurrent training uses swimming as the endurance mode in adults or masters.
- Exact effect sizes for Held 2026 and Huiberts on power and hypertrophy could not be verified, because full texts were blocked.
- The running-vs-cycling modality effect in Wilson 2012 is unverified.

## Q2. Session sequencing and spacing: same-day order, recovery hours, consecutive days, and hard vs easy swims

### Takeaway
Best supported:
- Separate swim and gym sessions by **at least 3 h, and preferably 6 h or more**. 24 h or more is best when strength or power is the priority.
- If both modes must go in one session, put **strength first**. This preserves lower-body dynamic strength (+6.9% vs the reverse order) and does not harm aerobic capacity.
- A **hard (very-heavy-intensity) swim set before upper-body lifting acutely cuts shoulder force output** (~23% lower internal-rotator torque).
- A dryland session **12 h before** a swim test did not impair 100 m sprint time, although maximal-strength work slightly reduced upper-body power the next morning.

### Cited Findings

**Order within a session**
- Eddens, van Someren & Howatson 2018 (Sports Med 48:177–188; 10 studies, ≥5-week programmes): resistance-then-endurance gave greater **lower-body dynamic strength** than the reverse order (weighted mean difference **6.91%**). — [Northumbria](https://researchportal.northumbria.ac.uk/en/publications/the-role-of-intra-session-exercise-sequence-on-the-interference-e/)
- Murlasits et al. 2018 (J Sports Sci; sequence meta-analysis): lower-body 1RM was significantly higher with strength-first order, and **sequence had no impact on aerobic capacity**. — [PubMed 28783467 via search](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28783467/)
- A 2023 sequence meta-analysis on VO2max and lower-limb strength also exists, but its numbers were not retrieved. — [PMC9908959](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9908959/)

**Spacing between sessions**
- Schumann 2022: explosive-strength attenuation was significant within the same session (p = 0.043) and not significant with ≥3 h separation. — [JCU](https://researchonline.jcu.edu.au/71546/)
- Robineau et al. 2016 (JSCR 30(3):672–683; 58 amateur rugby players; 7 weeks; 2 strength + 2 aerobic sessions/week; strength always first):
  - Bench press and half-squat 1RM gains were lower with **0 h** recovery than with 6 h, 24 h or strength-only.
  - Isokinetic knee-extension strength at 180°/s improved more with 24 h than with 0 h or 6 h.
  - VO2peak gains were greatest with **24 h**.
  - Authors' recommendation: avoid scheduling two "contradictory qualities with less than 6-hour recovery." — [LWW JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/03000/specific_training_effects_of_concurrent_aerobic.10.aspx); [FFR PDF](https://formation.ffr.fr/sites/default/files/documents/doc/2016-12/Specific%20training%20effects%20of%20concurrent%20aerobic%20and%20strength%20exercises%20depend%20on%20recovery%20duration.pdf)

**Swim-specific acute data**
- Arsoniadis et al. 2022 (Sports 10(3):29; review of concurrent resistance and swim training):
  - Acute resistance or swim training "may temporarily decrease subsequent muscle function," depending on the recovery between them. Studied same-unit gaps were only ~20–40 min.
  - **6 × 5 min front crawl at 105% of critical speed reduced maximal shoulder internal-rotator torque by ~23%.**
  - 60 min of running at 80% VO2max before resistance training cut force by ~20% compared with the reverse order.
  - Long-term resistance-then-swim data show improved front-crawl performance, upper- and lower-body max strength, and turn peak power, but the data are limited. — [MDPI doi](https://doi.org/10.3390/sports10030029)
- Tsoltos et al. 2023 (J Funct Morphol Kinesiol 8(3):87; n = 8; evening dryland of max strength or strength-endurance, then a 90-min swim, then a 100 m sprint 12 h later):
  - **No difference in 100 m time** (MS 64.70 s, SE 63.81 s, control 64.52 s), RPE, lactate or kinematics.
  - Jump height unchanged.
  - **Medicine-ball throw lower after MS than control** (4.44 vs 4.66 m, p < 0.05): upper-body function is still somewhat depressed at 12 h.
  - The authors cite a biphasic recovery after heavy loads (around 11 h and 11–22 h) and possible delayed potentiation up to 48 h after ≥85% 1RM priming. — [doi](https://doi.org/10.3390/jfmk8030087); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37489300/)

### Inferences (proposed planning rules; certainty low to moderate)
1. **Prefer separate days** for gym and swim. With 3 swims and 2 gym sessions there are enough days in a 7-day week: Robineau's 24 h condition was the best overall for both strength and VO2peak.
2. **If both fall on the same day, keep at least 6 h between them** (Robineau). An absolute floor is ≥3 h (Schumann). Avoid back-to-back sessions with only a 20–40 min gap.
3. **If they must be back-to-back, lift first.** This protects strength (Eddens, Murlasits) without harming aerobic fitness. One exception: for the injured shoulder, a short easy swim can serve as the warm-up before lifting. Avoid a hard swim set before heavy pulling or pressing because of the ~23% internal-rotator torque drop. This is an extrapolation.
4. **After a hard or threshold swim** (≥critical speed): do not schedule heavy upper-body work, such as pulling, pressing or rotator-cuff loading, within the next ~12–24 h. Lower-body, trunk or light corrective work is fine.
5. **After an easy aerobic swim**: next-day strength work is not compromised, and same-day strength work after ≥3–6 h is acceptable. This is extrapolated, because no data separate swim intensity from next-day strength.
6. **Before a key swim (a test or quality set)**: a heavy upper-body session the evening before (~12 h) is tolerable for swim time but blunts upper-body power. Ideally leave ≥24–48 h before a swim test.
7. **Consecutive days**: gym followed by an easy swim the next day is supported (Tsoltos: no performance decrement at 12 h). Avoid a heavy upper-body gym day immediately followed by a hard swim day, and the reverse.

### Gaps
- There is no masters- or older-adult-specific data on recovery spacing.
- No study compares low- vs high-intensity swim sessions for their effect on next-day strength training.
- Robineau's data come from young rugby players doing running and cycling, not swimming.

## Q3. Dryland strength training for swimmers: what transfers, which exercises, what dose

### Takeaway
Evidence for transfer of dryland strength to swim performance is **mixed and low quality** (small samples, mostly young or competitive male swimmers).
- The most consistent positive signals are for **maximal strength training** (low reps, high force or velocity) and for **combining** dryland resistance with swim training.
- Plyometrics mainly help starts and turns, which are irrelevant here and contraindicated for this user.
- Typical effective dose: **2 sessions/week for 6–8 or more weeks, 1–6 sets × 1–10 reps, 60–90 s rest.**
- For an injured shoulder, the best-supported add-on is external-rotator and scapular-retractor strengthening plus pectoral stretching for 6–8 weeks.

### Cited Findings

**Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of transfer**
- Crowley, Harrison & Lyons 2017 (Sports Med 47:2285–2307; 14 studies, 10 dryland and 4 swim-specific):
  - "Significant gaps" in evidence for transfer to swim performance.
  - Optimal transfer comes from "specific, low-volume, high-velocity/force" resistance programmes.
  - Stroke length is best improved by low-rep, high-velocity/force resistance training; resisted swims are best for stroke rate. — [University of Limerick](https://pure.ul.ie/en/publications/the-impact-of-resistance-training-on-swimming-performance-a-syste/); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Impact-of-Resistance-Training-on-Swimming-A-Crowley-Harrison/8e1603549f53c847876daf1d54dbdba52b96eb1f)
- Amaro et al. 2018/2019 (Science & Sports; 16 studies):
  - **7 of 16 studies found no positive or negative effect** on swimming performance.
  - Gains mostly appeared in older (non-child) swimmers, and **maximal strength training was most effective** for swim performance. Plyometrics were best for starts and turns.
  - Suggested regimen: **6–8 weeks, 2 sessions/week, 1–6 sets × 1–10 reps, 60–90 s rest.** — [Évora repository](https://www.rdpc.uevora.pt/handle/10174/24344); [oasisbr](https://oasisbr.ibict.br/vufind/Record/RCAP_8229384a971881920bbc36fd586e146b)
- Muniz-Pardos et al. 2019 (JSCR 33(10):2875–2881; swim-specific resistance training review): a later review summarises it as finding that swim-plus-strength protocols give "marginally greater" benefits than swim-only or strength-only training. Related work by the same group suggests that inertial and in-water resistance transfer better than traditional gym strength. — [Ovid](https://insights.ovid.com/strength-conditioning-research/jscr/2019/10/000/swim-specific-resistance-training-systematic/31/00124278); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/334660752_Swim-Specific_Resistance_Training_A_Systematic_Review)
- Fone & van den Tillaar 2022 (Sports Med Open 8:19; 27 studies; youth and experienced competitive swimmers; interventions longer than 3 weeks): "no clear consensus" on which strength-training method is best, and **all methods had at least one group that improved** swimming performance. — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-022-00410-5)
- 2025 two-tier network meta-analysis (Frontiers in Physiology, doi 10.3389/fphys.2025.1636595; 36 RCTs, 844 competitive swimmers):
  - **Combined aquatic + dryland training ranked first** for 25 m (SUCRA 85.2%), 50 m (97.2%), 100 m (99.5%), start (90.7%, not significant) and velocity (99.8%).
  - Combined vs control: 100 m time MD −2.01 s (95% CI −2.87 to −1.16); stroke rate SMD 1.63; stroke length SMD 0.86.
  - Combined training beat dryland-only on 100 m (MD −1.22 s).
  - In the second tier, **water + dryland resistance training (W+DRT) ranked highest**. Core and power training helped the 25 m sprint (MD −0.90 s) and take-off velocity. — [PubMed 40951636](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40951636/); [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/fe09/309516266da4c19bb78c5ba26caff1fb64ce.pdf)

**Concurrent swim + strength trials in swimmers**
- Garrido et al. 2010 (JSSM 9:300; young swimmers; 8 weeks of dryland strength + aerobic swimming):
  - Strength rose (6RM bench press higher than control, p < 0.05).
  - **No inhibition of swimming**, but only a trend toward improved sprint.
  - During detraining, strength held and swim performance kept improving. — [JSSM](https://www.jssm.org/jssm-09-300.xml%3EFulltext); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24149700/)
- A 2023 study tested two dryland approaches over 4 weeks plus a 2-week taper without dryland; neither improved water performance. — [PMC10464755](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10464755/)

**Shoulder-protective dryland work** (relevant to the user's rotator cuff/impingement)
- 2022 scoping review (J Sport Rehabil 31(5); 14 studies): 6–8 weeks of **external-rotator and scapular-retractor strengthening plus pectoral stretching** may reduce shoulder pain in swimmers. Exercise combined with manual therapy may help injured swimmers. — [Human Kinetics](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/jsr/31/5/article-p617.xml)
- RCT in adolescent competitive swimmers: dryland strengthening raised external-rotation strength compared with swim-only, but shoulder soreness did not differ. — [PMC4637920](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4637920/)
- RCT of elastic bands in a Kabat D2 diagonal pattern (n = 22, 8 weeks): improved external-rotation strength and rotator balance, but not significantly. — [PubMed 38028779](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38028779/)
- Rehabilitation progression for painful swimmer shoulder: resisted scapular retraction, then prone T and Y, then "field goals" (horizontal abduction with external rotation) as irritability falls. This is a case-level protocol. — [IJSPT](https://ijspt.scholasticahq.com/article/21234-a-novel-rehabilitation-program-using-neuromuscular-electrical-stimulation-nmes-and-taping-for-shoulder-pain-in-swimmers-a-protocol-and-case-example)

**In-season vs off-season dose and detraining**
- Coaching consensus (not peer-reviewed): about **2 sessions/week in-season (30–40 min, moderate loads, maintenance)** and 2–3 sessions/week off-season. — [swimsmarttoday](https://www.swimsmarttoday.com/pages/dryland-training); [skolfieldperformance](https://skolfieldperformance.com/training/dryland-for-swimmers/)
- Taper: keep the same exercises, cut volume (reported as down to ~25% of peak), and keep loads fairly heavy. — [SwimSwam](https://swimswam.com/dryland-taper-3-tips-to-generate-fast-swimming/); [USMS forum](https://community.usms.org/swimming/f/general/10293/dryland-training-during-taper)
- Detraining: a 5-week stop impaired dryland strength in trained swimmers (lower body in males, upper body in females). — [IJSPP 2022](https://journals.humankinetics.com/view/journals/ijspp/17/10/article-p1463.xml)
- A 12-week stop in elite swimmers deteriorated most land and water measures. — [PMC9030005](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9030005/)

### Inferences (for this user)
- **What to emphasize:**
  - Heavy-ish, low-rep **pulling** (lat pulldown or neutral-grip pull-down, seated cable row, straight-arm pulldown in pain-free range).
  - **Trunk/core** anti-extension and anti-rotation work.
  - **External-rotation and scapular work** (band ER at 0° abduction, prone Y/T, face pulls in pain-free range).
  - **Hip-dominant lower-body** work (hip hinge/RDL, hip thrust or bridge, leg press or box squat to ≤90°, step-ups at a low box height if the knee tolerates them).
  - Rationale: max strength has the best transfer signal (Amaro; Crowley), and ER/scapular work has the best shoulder signal.
- **What to exclude** (health filters, not evidence-based for performance):
  - Plyometrics, jumps and running drills (Perthes).
  - Deep loaded squats past 90°.
  - Overhead pressing and behind-the-neck pulling (impingement).
  - Deep or loaded knee flexion under compression, e.g. lunges and full squats (chondromalacia). Prefer limited-range leg press, hip-dominant patterns, and terminal knee extension.
- **Proposed dose:**
  - 2 sessions/week year-round.
  - Main lifts at 1–6 sets of 3–10 reps, around RPE 7–8 / RIR 2–3 for a 50+ trainee.
  - 60–90 s rest for accessories, longer for main lifts.
  - Deload or taper by cutting volume while keeping some heavy sets. Do not stop for more than about 2–3 weeks.
- Because a recreational swimmer's swim performance depends mainly on technique and aerobic fitness, the main gains from dryland work here are **health, sarcopenia prevention, injury resilience and shoulder balance**, more than stopwatch transfer. The network meta-analysis supports "swim + dryland" over swim-only for performance anyway.

### Gaps
- No pooled effect sizes exist for dryland → swim transfer in adults or masters. The network meta-analysis covered competitive (mostly young) swimmers, and its GRADE certainty could not be retrieved.
- The exact Fone & van den Tillaar method-by-method breakdown and Muniz-Pardos' conclusions were not retrieved.
- No peer-reviewed in-season vs off-season dose-response data for swimmers; frequency guidance is coaching consensus.
- No retrieved USA Swimming, British Swimming or NSCA position statement specific to swimmer dryland.

## Q4. Recommended weekly microcycles for swimmers with 2 strength sessions

### Takeaway
No RCT compares microcycle layouts (heavy/light, upper/lower split, and so on) for swimmers. Structure has to be built from general concurrent-training rules:
- separate days, or ≥6 h apart;
- strength before endurance if they share a session;
- ≥24 h between heavy upper-body work and hard swim sets;
- consistent with the common coaching pattern of two full-body sessions per week.

### Cited Findings
- Robineau 2016: a 2 strength + 2 aerobic sessions/week layout with **24 h** between modes gave the best combined strength and VO2peak outcomes. — [LWW JSCR](https://journals.lww.com/nsca-jscr/fulltext/2016/03000/specific_training_effects_of_concurrent_aerobic.10.aspx)
- Amaro: 2 dryland sessions/week. — [Évora](https://www.rdpc.uevora.pt/handle/10174/24344)
- Coaching sources: 2×/week in-season. — [swimsmarttoday](https://www.swimsmarttoday.com/pages/dryland-training)
- Held 2026 (secondary practical suggestions): separate sessions by 6–24 h; endurance ≤3 sessions/week reduces interference. — [Omnicuris](https://www.omnicuris.com/medshots/daily_updates/concurrent-training-adaptations-umbrella-review)
- Huiberts: upper-body strength is not blunted by concurrent training, so 2 upper-inclusive gym sessions alongside 3 swims are compatible in principle. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/374781428_Concurrent_Strength_and_Endurance_Training_A_Systematic_Review_and_Meta-Analysis_on_the_Impact_of_Sex_and_Training_Status)

### Inferences: candidate microcycle (extrapolated; low certainty)

| Day | Session |
|---|---|
| Mon | Swim A: quality/threshold (the hardest swim) |
| Tue | Gym A: "heavy" full-body. Lower-body hip-dominant main lifts heavy; upper pulling moderate; ER/scapular work; trunk |
| Wed | Swim B: easy/technique aerobic |
| Thu | Rest or mobility |
| Fri | Gym B: "light/upper-volume" full-body. Pulling emphasis at moderate load, shoulder-prehab volume; lower body lighter |
| Sat | Swim C: endurance/aerobic (longer, moderate) |
| Sun | Rest |

- **Why this layout:** every gym session is ≥24 h away from the hardest swim. No same-day doubles. Gym A (heaviest) follows the hard swim by 24 h and precedes an easy swim. Gym B precedes a moderate swim by ~24 h.
- **Alternative if days are scarce:** combine gym and an easy swim on the same day, gym first or ≥6 h apart.
- **Heavy/light (undulating) split** is preferred over upper/lower:
  - Upper/lower would put all shoulder loading into one day, which then risks colliding with swims.
  - Spreading pulling and ER/scapular work across both sessions at moderate volume suits the impingement better, and keeps per-session shoulder load low.
  - This is coaching logic, not trial evidence.
- **Shoulder-friendly rules:**
  - No overhead pressing; pulls in the scapular plane or with a neutral grip.
  - Pressing limited to landmine, incline (pain-free) or push-up variations.
  - ER and scapular work every session.
  - Pectoral stretching after swims.
  - Avoid heavy lat or shoulder fatigue the day before the hardest swim.

### Gaps
- No trial compares heavy/light vs upper/lower vs full-body splits in swimmers or masters swimmers.
- No evidence was found on "shoulder-friendly" programming as a whole package beyond the ER/scapular trials.

## Q5. Evidence specific to masters/older swimmers and concurrent training

### Takeaway
There are **no controlled trials of dryland or strength training in masters swimmers**, so this question rests on extrapolation:
- Concurrent-training meta-analyses in adults aged 50–73 show no interference, and strength gains greater than aerobic-only training.
- Schumann 2022 found age (>40 vs <40) did not moderate interference.
- Masters swim performance declines ~0.5–0.9%/year until about age 70, mainly through VO2max loss. Disuse explains part of the age-related decline.

### Cited Findings
- Masters 1-hour swim data (40 years): women's distance declines ~0.5%/year until about 69 years; men's ~0.9%/year until about 75; faster decline after that. — [doi 10.1080/11791543.2026.2641292](https://doi.org/10.1080/11791543.2026.2641292)
- Tanaka/Reaburn-type 40-year review: linear decline of 0.6–0.7%/year until about 70, then exponential decline. VO2max loss is the main driver. Many "age" changes are partly disuse and less pronounced in well-conditioned masters athletes. — [PMC3781853](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3781853/)
- Female masters swimmers (n = 73, ages 24–71) were stronger than sedentary peers but still showed an age-related strength decline (1985). — [Tandfonline](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/02701367.1985.10608442)
- Middle-aged and older adults (50+): concurrent training ≥ single-mode for strength and cardiorespiratory fitness, with no interference; greater lean mass than aerobic-only. — [PMC9876872](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9876872/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0031938422001949); [MDPI 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2227-9032/13/7/776)
- Schumann 2022: mean age <40 vs >40 did not moderate concurrent-training effects. — [JCU](https://portfolio.jcu.edu.au/publications/JCU174136)

### Inferences
- For a 50+ swimmer, the cost of **not** strength training (sarcopenia, strength loss that swimming alone does not stop) likely outweighs any interference risk. Swimming alone did not prevent age-related strength decline in masters women (1985 study).
- **Recovery is plausibly slower with age** (general knowledge, not retrieved here). This supports the ≥24 h spacing, the heavy/light alternation, and RIR 2–3 rather than training to failure.
- The detraining data suggest avoiding gym breaks of more than about 2–3 weeks.

### Gaps
- No RCT, cohort or meta-analysis of resistance or dryland training in masters swimmers measuring swim performance, injury or recovery.
- No masters-specific data on session spacing or sequencing.
- No retrieved US Masters Swimming or World Aquatics guidance on dryland dosing.
