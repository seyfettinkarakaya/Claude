# Periodization for a 50+ masters swimmer with concurrent gym strength training (integrated season: tissue adaptation → base → build → taper, Bosphorus open-water swim in August)

Method note: web searches only; full-text fetches of PMC, Frontiers, SHU repository and Waikato were blocked by the egress proxy, so most findings come from abstracts/search snippets and are cited to the landing pages. Items marked [UNVERIFIED] are from prior knowledge and were not confirmed in this session — encode them as defaults, not hard rules.

## 1. Block vs traditional/linear vs undulating periodization — comparative evidence

### Takeaway
No periodization model is clearly superior for endurance performance; block periodization (BP) shows small pooled advantages in VO2max/Wmax from small, low-quality studies, and later load-matched trials found no difference. For strength, periodized > non-periodized (moderate effect, mainly in trained people), but linear vs undulating show no difference. For a recreational masters athlete the evidence supports "any sensible, progressive, periodized plan" — choose the model for practicality and recovery management, not superiority.

### Cited Findings
- Endurance BP meta-analysis (Mølmen, Øfsteng, Rønnestad 2019, Open Access J Sports Med): 20 records eligible, 6 pooled; small favorable effect of BP vs traditional (TRAD) on VO2max (SMD 0.40; 95% CI 0.02–0.79) and Wmax (SMD 0.28; 0.01–0.54); mean PEDro 3.7/10, authors urge caution — [Dovepress/OAJSM](https://www.dovepress.com/block-periodization-of-endurance-training-a-systematic-review-and-meta-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-OAJSM); [PMC6802561](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6802561)
- Note: the requested "Moesgaard 2022" is a *strength/hypertrophy* meta-analysis, not endurance BP (see below). The endurance BP meta-analysis is Mølmen 2019 — [search record](https://sponet.de/Record/4062075)
- Rønnestad block design (well-trained cyclists, 12 wk): BP = every 4th week had 5 HIT sessions, followed by 3 weeks with 1 HIT/week; TRAD = 2 HIT/week, matched total HIT; HIT sessions were 6×5 min or 5×6 min hard intervals with ~2.5–3 min recoveries — [Joe Friel blog summary](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4089050-another-block-periodization-study); [TrainingPeaks plan citing the study](https://www.trainingpeaks.com/training-plans/cycling/tp-156954/4wk-block-periodized-aka-front-loaded-hiit)
- Reported BP gains in that work: VO2max +4.6%, power at VO2max +2.1%, power at ~aerobic threshold +10% vs no change in TRAD (secondary summary) — [Joe Friel blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4089050-another-block-periodization-study)
- Almquist et al. 2022 (Frontiers in Physiology; 25 well-trained cyclists, 12 wk, identical volume of low/moderate/high intensity): both groups improved, no significant differences between BP and TP; authors note most BP vs TP studies are short — [Frontiers PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/physiology/articles/10.3389/fphys.2022.837634/pdf)
- Systematic review of trained cyclists (2023): no evidence favoring a specific periodization model over 8–12 weeks — [Sponet record](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4080476?lng=en)
- Friel (coach, low evidence weight): block plans may suit advanced athletes; focusing on 1–2 abilities per mesocycle can lose other fitness qualities — [Joe Friel blog](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/4089050-another-block-periodization-study)
- Williams et al. 2017 (Sports Med): periodized resistance training has a moderate effect on 1RM vs non-periodized; larger gains in untrained, with higher frequency and longer study duration; snippets suggest undulating favorable for strength (subgroup numbers not verified) — [PDF](https://www.fisiologiadelejercicio.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/05/Comparison-of-Periodized-and-Non-Periodized-Resistance.pdf)
- Harries et al. 2015 (JSCR): 17 studies, 510 participants — no difference between linear and undulating periodization for upper- or lower-body strength; short study durations and training history are confounders — [PubMed 25268290](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25268290/)
- Moesgaard et al. 2022 (Sports Med 52:1647–1666; 35 RCTs, 1,187 participants mostly beginners, 6–36 wk, 2–4 sessions/wk, volume-equated): periodization benefited maximal strength only in *trained* participants; no detectable effect on hypertrophy; strength benefit likely neural — [PubMed 35044672](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35044672/); [sci-sport summary](https://sci-sport.com/en/effects-intensity-volume-periodization-strength-hypertrophy-238/) (secondary summary reports ES ≈0.31 for strength — not verified against original)
- Issurin block concept: blocks are sequenced so earlier gains persist via "residual training effects" while a new focus is trained (Issurin 2010, Sports Med 40:189–206) — [SDSU coach science summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol161/issurin.htm); [Sportlyzer wiki](https://academy.sportlyzer.com/wiki/residual-training-effect)
- Residual durations commonly attributed to Issurin (secondary, inconsistent): aerobic endurance ~30±5 days, max strength ~30±5 days, anaerobic glycolytic ~18±4 days, explosive/max speed ~5±3 days — [Fitbudd](https://www.fitbudd.com/academy/block-periodization-definition-types-uses-and-examples-complete-guide); another blog gives aerobic 25–30 d, strength 15–20 d — [elitefts](https://www.elitefts.com/education/training/block-periodization-in-the-sport-of-powerlifting) (WEAK: verify Table 1 of Issurin 2010)

### Inferences
- For the app: do not hard-code model superiority. A pragmatic hybrid fits: traditional macro sequence (general → specific) with 4-week mesocycles (3 load + 1 deload), and within-week (weekly undulating) variation in gym loading. Masters athletes are "recreational/trained" — Moesgaard suggests some undulation helps strength in trained lifters, but magnitude is small.
- Concentrated HIT blocks (Rønnestad 5 HIT in one week) are high-risk for a 50+ athlete with shoulder impingement; prefer the "traditional" 2 HIT/week distribution or a softened block (max 3 HIT sessions/week in a shock week). Inference, no masters data.
- Residuals (~30 d aerobic/strength) justify a rule: never let a quality (max strength, aerobic base) go untrained > ~3–4 weeks; maintain with 1 session/week.

### Gaps
- No RCT comparing periodization models in masters/older athletes or masters swimmers was found.
- Williams 2017 linear vs undulating subgroup effect sizes not verified (full text not accessible).
- Issurin residual table exact values unverified.

## 2. Swimming-specific intensity distribution (polarized vs pyramidal vs threshold), CSS zones, and phase changes

### Takeaway
Evidence in swimmers is small and mostly elite/junior. Polarized TID gave small 100 m benefits with less fatigue vs threshold TID; pyramidal TID favored 800 m in adolescents; distance swimmers observationally train pyramidal. For a long open-water goal (~6.5 km Bosphorus), a pyramidal distribution (~70–80% easy, ~15–25% threshold/CSS, ~5% fast) is best supported, with polarization of the fast component closer to race-specific work.

### Cited Findings
- Pla et al. 2019 (IJSPP, doi 10.1123/ijspp.2018-0179): 22 elite junior swimmers, crossover, two 6-wk periods. POL = 81/4/15% (Z1/Z2/Z3), THR = 65/25/10%. POL gave small-to-moderately greater 100 m improvement, less fatigue, better recovery quality; no clear difference in physiological adaptations — [HAL-INSEP](https://hal-insep.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-02936224); [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4053138?lng=en)
- 12-wk pyramidal vs polarized in national adolescent female swimmers: pyramidal reduced 800 m time more; polarized reduced 100 m time more; both increased VO2max (small sample) — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4075910)
- González-Ravé et al. 2021 systematic review (elite swimmers): sprinters → polarized/threshold TID; middle-distance → threshold/pyramidal; long-distance → primarily pyramidal; mostly observational, unclear whether other approaches would be better — [UAX repository](https://prepositorio.uax.com/items/255da3b9-94f3-4a25-bcef-137ecdb0df5e/full); [Canberra record](https://researchprofiles.canberra.edu.au/en/publications/training-intensity-distribution-training-volume-and-periodization/)
- HIIT in competitive swimmers meta-analysis: preliminary evidence that pyramidal approaches may help ≥800 m events; polarized suggested for 200–400 m — [ECU record](https://ro.ecu.edu.au/ecuworks2022-2026/8057)
- Coaching commentary: polarized training not yet established as preferred for swimmers — [SwimISCA](https://read.swimisca.org/?p=40333) (low evidence weight)
- Hydren & Cohen 2015 (JSCR review on polarized training) — content NOT retrieved; see Gaps.
- CSS test: 400 m + 200 m all-out; CSS (s/100 m) = (T400 − T200)/2; roughly the pace sustainable ~1 h — [TopEndSports](https://www.topendsports.com/testing/tests/critical-swim-speed.htm); [ctyeh](https://ctyeh.com/articles/2795?lang=en)
- CSS tends to sit slightly above maximal lactate steady state ("slightly aggressive" threshold); one coach uses threshold = CSS ±2 s/100 m — [FwdMotion newsletter](https://fwdmotionsthlm-newsletter.beehiiv.com/p/css-may-not-be-what-you-think-it-is)
- Zone schemes differ (all as % of CSS pace time, higher % = slower): e.g. Z1 110–115%, Z2 105–110%, Z3 100–105%, Z4 95–100%, Z5 <95% — [ctyeh](https://ctyeh.com/articles/2795?lang=en); alternative: recovery >115%, endurance 105–115%, threshold 95–105%, VO2max 85–95%, sprint <85% — [calculover](https://calculover.com/health-fitness/fitness-performance/swim-pace/) (coaching sources, low weight)
- Open-water pace typically 5–8 s/100 m slower than pool; retest CSS every 6–8 weeks; use trends, not single tests — [ctyeh](https://ctyeh.com/articles/2795?lang=en)

### Inferences
- Rule set (inferred; practical defaults): 3-zone model anchored to CSS seconds/100 m:
  - Z1 easy/aerobic: slower than CSS +6 s/100 m (≈ >105% CSS pace time).
  - Z2 threshold: CSS −2 to +4 s/100 m.
  - Z3 fast/VO2/sprint: faster than CSS −3 s/100 m.
- TID by phase (time-in-zone, inferred from the above):
  - Phase 0 tissue adaptation: ~90/8/2 (technique, drills, easy aerobic; short pick-ups only).
  - Phase 1 aerobic base: ~80/15/5 (pyramidal).
  - Phase 2 build/threshold: ~70–75/20–25/5 (pyramidal, CSS sets e.g. 10–20×100 at CSS r10–20 s).
  - Phase 3 specific/race prep (open water): ~75/20/5 with long continuous swims at race pace (≈CSS +3–6 s) and some open-water sessions.
  - Taper: keep the intensity distribution; cut volume (see §5).
- Since the goal is long open water, emphasize pyramidal; include some Z3 throughout for masters (see §6) rather than eliminating it.
- Retest CSS at the start of each mesocycle or every 6–8 weeks (after deload week, when fresh).

### Gaps
- No TID study in masters or recreational adult swimmers found.
- Hydren & Cohen 2015 findings not retrieved; no swim-specific data on what fraction should be "threshold" for 5–10 km open water in amateurs.
- Precise CSS–MLSS offset varies; zone boundaries are coaching convention, not validated.

## 3. Nesting strength periodization inside swim phases (concurrent training)

### Takeaway
Dry-land strength (especially maximal strength and low-volume high-force work) transfers modestly to swimming; reviews of endurance athletes support a sequence of strength-endurance/anatomical adaptation → basic/max strength → power/maintenance, 2×/week in preparation and 1–2×/week in season, ≥48 h apart. Sequencing within a day matters little for endurance outcomes. Age may increase interference, so more recovery spacing is prudent.

### Cited Findings
- Crowley et al. 2017 (as summarized in a 2022 Sports Med-Open paper): 14 studies (10 dry-land, 4 swim-specific resistance); low-volume, high-velocity/force, swim-specific resistance training showed positive transfer; methodological quality low; no effect-size analysis — [Springer s40798-022-00410-5](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40798-022-00410-5)
- Amaro et al. 2018 (Science & Sports): 16 studies, 7 found no effect on swim performance; maximal strength suggested as most effective — [LIDA record](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4055835); [Évora PDF](https://dspace.uevora.pt/rdpc/bitstream/10174/24344/1/1-s2.0-S0765159718302363-main.pdf)
- Dry-land strength, ballistic and plyometric (neural) training improves turn performance — [PMC8431432](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8431432/) (plyometrics contraindicated here — see constraints)
- Endurance-athlete strength review (2015): heavy, high-force low-velocity training recommended first for those with limited strength experience; a sequenced approach of strength-endurance → basic strength → strength → power for higher-level athletes — [LIDA record "Strength training for endurance athletes: Theory to practice"](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4034811)
- Runners/cyclists review (2024): concurrent strength gives moderate improvements in economy without changing VO2max; interventions 6–14 wk, maintenance phases up to 15 wk; resistance training 2×/week pre-season and 1–2×/week in-season, ≥48 h apart — [EBSCO record](https://www.ebsco.com/articles/sports-and-leisure/d861a7ab-376a-56f0-8487-04d5e190b01f/strength-training-for-previously-trained-endurance-runners-and-cyclists)
- Sequencing: strength-first optimizes neuromuscular/explosive adaptations; sequence shows no consistent association with endurance, hypertrophy or max-strength gains — [PMC12885173](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12885173/); ER vs RE sequence effects small/non-conclusive for endurance — [LIDA 4089557](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4089557)
- Concurrent training in team sports: 9/24 studies found stronger interference in older and more experienced players; extended recovery between sessions and sequencing may reduce it — [LIDA 4083683](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4083683?lng=en)
- Masters swimmers train less and focus on endurance, not strength, which may partly explain age-related decline (UBC study) — [UBC MSL](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/224)

### Inferences
- Nested strength calendar (inferred rules):
  - Phase 0 (4–6 wk) Anatomical adaptation: 2–3×/wk, 2–3 sets × 12–15 reps, RIR 3–4, tempo-controlled, scapular/rotator-cuff and hip-stability emphasis.
  - Phase 1 base → hypertrophy/strength-endurance: 2×/wk, 3 × 8–12, RIR 2–3.
  - Phase 2 build → max strength: 2×/wk, 3–4 × 4–6 (≈80–85% 1RM), RIR 2; long rests. Masters benefit most here (counter fast-twitch loss).
  - Phase 3 specific → power/maintenance: 1–2×/wk, 2–3 × 3–5 fast concentric (≤ moderate load, no jumps), volume −30–40%.
  - Taper: 1 short session in week −2, none or activation only in race week (keep load, cut sets ~50%).
- Scheduling rules: heavy lower-body strength not within 24 h before key swim sessions (threshold/long swim); same-day: swim key session AM, strength PM (≥6 h) or strength first only when the strength goal is primary (Phase 2). Two strength days ≥48 h apart.
- Health filter: no jumps/plyometrics/running (Perthes); replace squats with hip-friendly patterns (box squat to above parallel, leg press limited ROM, hip thrust, step-ups low box, sled push/pull); limit knee flexion under load for chondromalacia (avoid deep knee flexion, prefer partial ROM 0–60°, leg extension avoided at high load in end range); shoulder impingement: avoid overhead pressing and behind-neck, prefer landmine press, neutral-grip rows, external rotation, scapular retraction, limit paddles and butterfly volume.
- "BR ≤10%/month" constraint → weekly load progression capped: swim volume +≤10% per 4-week block (≈2.5%/week) and strength volume similar.

### Gaps
- No studies of concurrent swim+strength periodization in masters swimmers found.
- Optimal strength frequency for 50+ swimmers not established; recommendations extrapolated from younger endurance athletes and general aging guidance.

## 4. Deload/recovery weeks: frequency, magnitude

### Takeaway
Deloading is ubiquitous but under-researched; the only formal source is an expert Delphi (Bell et al. 2023) for strength/physique sports. It defines a deload as a period of reduced training stress to mitigate fatigue and enhance preparedness. Frequency/magnitude specifics (commonly ~every 4–8 weeks, ~1 week, reduce volume, maintain load/intensity) are expert opinion, not trial evidence. For older athletes, 2:1 or 3:1 cycling is a coaching heuristic with no direct RCT support.

### Cited Findings
- Bell, Strafford, Coleman, Androulakis Korakakis, Nolan 2023 (Sports Med-Open, doi 10.1186/s40798-023-00633-0): 3-round online Delphi; 34 → 29 → 21 coaches; 138 statements in 4 domains; deloading ubiquitous yet under-researched; consensus definition: "a period of reduced training stress designed to mitigate physiological and psychological fatigue, promote recovery, and enhance preparedness for subsequent training" — [PMC10511399](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10511399/); [DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/599784d71dff41b197ff5cf66a3d11dd)
- Follow-up practical paper (Bell et al., Strength Cond J 2025): deloads implemented by reducing intensity of effort, training volume, duration or frequency — [SHU record](https://shura.shu.ac.uk/35313/)
- Bosquet 2007 taper data (§5) indicate that short-term volume cuts of 41–60% with maintained intensity improve performance — a mechanistic anchor for deload design — [PubMed 17762369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/)
- Masters recovery: older athletes may experience more muscle damage and/or slower repair; recovery-rate declines estimated to begin ~age 40 after resistance exercise (Fell & Williams 2008, J Aging Phys Act 16:97–115) — [UTAS eCite](https://ecite.utas.edu.au/44946)
- Cycling-focused summary: masters seem to recover physically about as quickly as young athletes, but take longer to *feel* recovered — [Fast Talk Labs](https://kadence.fasttalklabs.com/staying-fit-and-fast-as-a-masters-athlete/) (low evidence weight); see also [Sports Performance Bulletin](https://www.sportsperformancebulletin.com/training/masters/older-athletes-dont-get-sore-get-faster)

### Inferences
- App rules (defaults, expert-opinion level):
  - Mesocycle = 4 weeks, 3:1 (load/deload) by default; offer 2:1 when age ≥55, during max-strength phase, or when readiness markers (soreness, sleep, shoulder pain score, RPE drift) are flagged 2+ times in a week. Autoregulated early deload trigger overrides calendar.
  - Deload magnitude: volume −30 to −50% (swim metres and gym sets), keep intensity (pace zones and load on bar) but reduce effort (gym RIR +2, fewer reps); keep frequency or drop 1 session.
  - Place CSS/strength retests at the end of the deload week.
- Progression across load weeks inside a mesocycle: e.g. 100% → 105% → 110% → 60–70% (deload), constrained by the ≤10%/month cap.

### Gaps
- Bell 2023 full-text consensus statements (frequency in weeks, % volume reduction, agreement levels) could not be retrieved [UNVERIFIED memory: panel favored ~every 4–8 weeks, ~1 week, reduced volume and reduced intensity of effort; treat as unconfirmed].
- No controlled trial found comparing 2:1 vs 3:1 loading in older/masters athletes.

## 5. Tapering for swimmers and open-water events

### Takeaway
Best evidence (Bosquet 2007 meta-analysis): ~2-week taper, volume reduced exponentially/progressively by 41–60%, intensity and frequency maintained. An endurance-only meta-analysis (2023) suggests up to ~21 days with the same volume rule. Open-water coaching sources favor shorter (≈1 wk) tapers emphasizing endurance maintenance — low-evidence.

### Cited Findings
- Bosquet et al. 2007 (MSSE 39:1358–1365): 27 of 182 studies; optimal = 2-wk taper (ES 0.59 ±0.33), volume decreased exponentially by 41–60% (ES 0.72 ±0.36), without modifying intensity (ES 0.33 ±0.14) or frequency (ES 0.35 ±0.17) — [PubMed 17762369](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17762369/); [SDSU summary](https://coachsci.sdsu.edu/csa/vol131/bosquet.htm)
- Wang et al. 2023 (PLOS ONE, endurance athletes only, 14 studies): recommends ≤21-day taper with progressive volume reduction of 41–60%, intensity and frequency unchanged; notes Bosquet included sprinters — [PMC10171681](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10171681/); [Sponet record](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4081751?lng=en)
- Secondary summary interpretation: 8–14 days appears ideal (not exact wording of Bosquet abstract) — [Stronger by Science](https://www.strongerbyscience.com/tapering/)
- Mujika chapters on swim tapering exist (Pyne & Mujika 2011 in World Book of Swimming; Mujika & Stewart 2015 in Science of Swimming Faster) — content not retrieved — [EHU record](https://ekoizpen-zientifikoa.ehu.eus/documentos/5f1cd9b829995265e44d2c6d?lang=de); [VU record](https://vuir.vu.edu.au/view/people/Riewald=3AS=3A=3A.html)
- Open-water taper (coaching article): must maintain endurance; ~1 week often sufficient for endurance-trained swimmers; keep steady aerobic efforts rather than short sprint bursts — [Active.com](https://www.active.com/articles/endurance-swimmers-tips-for-tapering-for-an-open-water-race) (low evidence weight)
- Research on open water is concentrated on elites; amateurs understudied — [hrcak](https://hrcak.srce.hr/348449)

### Inferences
- Taper rules for Bosphorus (~6.5 km, downstream current) — inferred:
  - Length 10–14 days (masters may tolerate the longer end; volume-heavy prior phase justifies 2 weeks).
  - Week −2: swim volume −30 to −40%; week −1 (race week): −50 to −60% of pre-taper average; exponential, not step.
  - Keep frequency (same number of swims), keep some race-pace and short Z3 efforts (e.g. 6–8×50 fast, 3×400 race pace).
  - Strength: last heavy session ~10–12 days out; one light/maintenance session week −2; mobility/activation only in race week.
  - Last long open-water swim (≥ race distance or ~80%) ~2–3 weeks before.

### Gaps
- No taper studies specific to masters swimmers or to 5–10 km amateur open-water events found.
- Mujika's swim-specific taper chapters not accessible.

## 6. Aging/masters-specific considerations

### Takeaway
Masters swim performance declines ~0.6%/yr to age ~70 then accelerates; masters typically under-train strength and high intensity relative to younger swimmers; maintaining intensity and adding strength work likely mitigates part of the decline. Recovery may be slower (perceptually at least), arguing for more recovery spacing, not for eliminating intensity.

### Cited Findings
- National-champion masters swimmers: linear decline ~0.6%/yr up to ~70, exponential acceleration beyond, with more variability — [Tanaka/Seals-type review via OAJSM 2010 / DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/231da2724ca94125ac22e4e8ebc0be66)
- Brazilian championship data: peaks at 25–34 age groups; decline 0.6%/yr (50 free men) to 1.4%/yr (100 fly women) — [USP dissertation](https://teses.usp.br/teses/disponiveis/39/39132/tde-10092021-133932/?lang=en)
- Water training ≈80% of masters sessions, ~70% of it aerobic; authors hypothesize balanced strength/flexibility/coordination could slow aging (hypothesis only) — [USP dissertation](https://teses.usp.br/teses/disponiveis/39/39132/tde-10092021-133932/?lang=en)
- Masters swimmers spend less time training and focus on endurance, not strength, vs younger elites — [UBC MSL](https://msl.kin.educ.ubc.ca/node/224)
- Most masters swim research is cross-sectional; only one longitudinal study identified in a biomechanics/energetics review — [UBI repository](https://ubibliorum.ubi.pt/bitstreams/ce74280f-c0b2-4979-9f88-cd8338618825/download)
- Practical over-70 adjustments: longer warm-ups, longer rests between sets, less distance, more time between workouts — [Calgary Herald via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/canada/calgary-herald/20160723/283180082820911) (low evidence weight)
- Older cyclists gained more strength than younger ones from 3 weeks of added strength training; age-related fast-twitch strength loss may be partly "use it or lose it" — [Fast Talk Labs](https://kadence.fasttalklabs.com/staying-fit-and-fast-as-a-masters-athlete/) (secondary)
- Muscle recovery in older athletes possibly impaired (greater damage, slower repair) — [UTAS eCite, Fell & Williams 2008](https://ecite.utas.edu.au/44946); narrative review of masters recovery kinetics (Borges et al. 2016) notes evidence relied on animal and non-athlete comparisons — [Waikato record](https://researchcommons.waikato.ac.nz/entities/publication/43c52ed0-3e7e-4f32-98fa-ccd16580054b)
- Concealment effect: increasing training volume/intensity can mask age-related decline for a while — [USMS Swimmaster 1976](https://cd3.usms.org/hist/swimmaster/1976_swimmaster_v5n8_10.pdf) (historical, low weight)
- Accumulated injuries (e.g., knee arthritis, back problems) often limit older athletes' training — [Fast Talk Labs](https://kadence.fasttalklabs.com/staying-fit-and-fast-as-a-masters-athlete/)

### Inferences
- Masters rules for the app:
  - Keep ≥1 high-intensity swim stimulus/week in all phases except Phase 0 (short Z3 reps, full recovery) — counters fast-twitch and VO2max decline.
  - Keep strength year-round (minimum 1×/wk maintenance; 2×/wk in development phases).
  - Hard days separated by ≥48 h (≥72 h for heavy lower-body strength + hard swim combos); no more than 2–3 "hard" sessions (swim Z2/Z3 key set or heavy gym) per week.
  - Weekly microcycle template (example for 5 swims + 2 gym): Mon swim easy/technique; Tue swim CSS key + gym upper (pull/scapular); Wed off or easy swim; Thu swim Z3 short + gym lower (hip/knee-friendly); Fri easy; Sat long swim (open water from ~May); Sun off.
  - Readiness gating: shoulder pain ≥3/10 → replace paddles/fly/high-volume freestyle with kick/backstroke-friendly sets; knee/hip pain → drop breaststroke kick and loaded knee flexion.
- Phase calendar (example backward from Bosphorus mid/late August; inferred):
  - Phase 0 tissue adaptation: Oct–Nov (6–8 wk).
  - Phase 1 aerobic base: Dec–Feb (3 × 4-wk mesocycles).
  - Phase 2 build/threshold + max strength: Mar–May (3 mesocycles).
  - Phase 3 specific/open-water: Jun–Jul (2 mesocycles, open-water acclimatization, long swims building to ≥ race distance).
  - Taper: last 10–14 days of August build-up; race; transition 2–4 weeks easy/active rest.

### Gaps
- Tanaka & Seals' masters swimming paper (decline rates, J Appl Physiol 1997) and Reaburn's masters-athlete recovery work could not be accessed directly; decline figures come from a search snippet of an OAJSM 2010 review.
- No evidence on optimal training frequency for 50+ masters swimmers; no masters-specific deload or taper trials.
- Health-constraint adaptations (Perthes, impingement, chondromalacia) are clinical-practice inferences, not drawn from studies found here — should be checked with a physiotherapist.
